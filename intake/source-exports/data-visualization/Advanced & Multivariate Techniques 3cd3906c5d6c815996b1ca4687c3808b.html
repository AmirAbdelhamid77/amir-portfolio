Advanced & Multivariate Techniques

Purpose: Techniques for when the analytical question outgrows two variables — encoding a third dimension, screening many continuous variables at once, and handling genuinely cyclical data.

12. Advanced Multivariate Visualization — Higher Dimensions

When the analytical question involves more than two variables, do not jump automatically to 3D charts. A 3D object rendered on a 2D screen often introduces perspective and occlusion problems without improving comparison.

flowchart TD
A[Need a third variable?] --> B{Is it categorical or binned?}
B -->|Yes| F[Facet / small multiples]
B -->|No, continuous| C{Must the value be precise?}
C -->|Yes| P[Keep X/Y as the two most important variables. Add color carefully.]
C -->|No| S[Color, then size. Opacity last. Avoid 3D.]
F --> D[Watch cognitive load as panel count grows]

12.1 Adding a Third Dimension

A scatter plot can encode additional variables through visual aesthetics:

  • Position (X/Y): usually the strongest and most precise encodings.
  • Color: powerful for a third variable, especially when the scale is appropriate.
  • Size: possible, but large marks can dominate attention and overlap.
  • Transparency (alpha): useful for density/overplotting, but precise values are difficult to judge.
  • Shape: useful mainly for categorical groups; poor for ordered quantitative values because shapes have no natural magnitude ordering.
  • Facets/panels: often the clearest way to split a third variable into meaningful groups.

Real use case: country development

For UN-style country data:

  • X = years of schooling
  • Y = GNI per capita
  • Color = life expectancy

This reveals three variables in one 2D view and may also expose the positive association between schooling and GNI. The trade-off is that exact life-expectancy values become difficult to read from color alone.

12.2 Why Size and Transparency Are Weaker Encodings

Humans generally judge position more accurately than area or opacity. Therefore, size and transparency are better treated as secondary encodings rather than replacements for precise axes when exact comparison matters.

12.3 Faceting: the Controlled Alternative

Faceting creates separate panels for subsets of the data. It is especially useful when the third variable is categorical or can be meaningfully binned.

Example:

Compare countries with life expectancy 50–60, 60–70, and 70–80 years in separate panels, then compare the distributions of schooling and GNI across groups.

More/smaller panels can increase precision but also increase eye movement and cognitive load. There is therefore a trade-off between precision and scanning cost.

13. Plotting Many Variables at Once

13.1 Pair Plot

Use a pair plot when you have a relatively small set of variables (roughly up to ten) and want a rapid EDA view of:

  1. Each variable's distribution.
  1. Every pairwise relationship.

Anatomy

  • Diagonal: distributions. Categorical variables may appear as count/bar displays; continuous variables commonly appear as histograms.
  • Off-diagonal: pairwise relationships. Continuous × continuous commonly appears as scatter plots; categorical × continuous can appear as box plots or grouped distributions.

Real use case: LA housing

For variables such as city, beds, price, and area, a pair plot can quickly reveal:

  • Which cities have different price distributions.
  • Whether area and price are positively associated.
  • Whether beds and area are correlated.
  • Potential outliers or unusual clusters.

13.2 Correlation Heatmap

A correlation heatmap is essentially a compact representation of pairwise relationships among many continuous variables. Instead of drawing every scatter plot, correlation strength/direction is represented by color.

Best use

Use it as a screening tool to find strongly related variables among tens of metrics.

Critical limitation

A heatmap of Pearson correlations primarily summarizes linear association. It cannot replace inspecting scatter plots because nonlinear relationships can have weak linear correlation.

Real use case: customer satisfaction

If customers rate multiple product features from 1–10, a heatmap can reveal groups of features that customers tend to rate similarly—for example, several price-related attributes may form a highly correlated block.

13.3 Parallel Coordinates

Parallel coordinates are useful when you have many continuous variables and want to inspect profiles, patterns, or clusters.

Each observation becomes a line crossing one vertical axis per variable. Variables are placed along the X-axis, and each variable is scaled so observations can be compared across dimensions.

Real use case: country development profiles

Add GNI, HDI, life expectancy, schooling, and additional development metrics. Splitting/coloring by continent can reveal profile differences:

  • Europe: generally high non-income development metrics with wider GNI variation.
  • Africa: generally lower GNI with wider variation in other indicators.
  • South America: comparatively consistent profiles across several indicators.

Strength

You can add many variables—ten or twenty is feasible—without creating a pairwise matrix.

Weakness

With too many observations, line crossings create a visual "spaghetti" effect. Grouping, filtering, transparency, ordering variables carefully, and highlighting selected observations can help.

14. Polar Coordinates, Pie Charts, and Rose Plots

14.1 Pie Chart = Bar Chart in Polar Coordinates

Conceptually, converting a bar chart from Cartesian coordinates to polar coordinates turns bar lengths into angles. The resulting pie chart is generally harder to compare precisely than a bar chart.

Default rule: prefer a bar chart when the analytical goal is accurate comparison of category magnitudes.

flowchart TD
P{Does the variable have natural circularity?} -->|No| Bar[Bar / dot — not a pie]
P -->|Yes: clock, compass, season| Polar[Polar / rose is defensible]

14.2 When Polar Coordinates Make Sense

Polar coordinates are defensible when the underlying variable has natural circularity:

  • Time of day.
  • Direction/compass angle.
  • Other genuinely cyclical measurements.

14.3 Rose Plot

A rose plot can be understood as a histogram mapped onto polar coordinates. This is useful when the beginning and end of a linear axis are actually adjacent in the phenomenon.

Real use case: animal activity

A nocturnal animal may be active around midnight. On a conventional histogram, activity just before and after midnight can appear split at opposite ends of the x-axis. A polar representation joins the cycle, making the overnight activity burst easier to recognize.
